# WordQuest six-word teaching preview

WQ-3B-SAMPLE-0.1.0

Original candidate content; not imported into the live curriculum.

## crystal

countable noun · /ˈkrɪstəl/

**Definition:** A solid whose tiny parts are arranged in a regular, repeating pattern.

**Objective:** Understand a crystal as an ordered solid, rather than any shiny stone.

**Original examples**

- A small crystal grew at the bottom of the jar.
- Under the microscope, the salt crystal looked like a tiny cube.
- The explorers found a quartz crystal inside the cave.

**Collocations**

- a quartz crystal
- a salt crystal
- grow crystals
- a crystal structure

**Word family and forms**

- crystal → crystals (plural)
- crystallize (verb): to form crystals; American spelling uses -ize
- crystalline (adjective): having a crystal structure

**Watch out:** A crystal is not simply a shiny stone. A rock can contain several minerals, and a crystal can also be made in a lab. Crystal clear is a figurative expression, not this noun meaning.

**Listening focus:** First-syllable stress; keep the /st/ cluster and the final /l/ clear.

**Visual proposal:** An original diagram comparing a salt cube, a quartz crystal and a mixed rock; include a repeating-particle inset, labelled as a model rather than an actual photograph.

**Science extension proposal:** Compare cube-shaped salt crystals with quartz crystals. Shape depends on the repeating internal structure and on growth conditions. Suggest a short time-lapse of safe salt-crystal growth with adult supervision; no video is bundled.

## happy

adjective · /ˈhæpi/

**Definition:** Feeling pleased or glad about something.

**Objective:** Describe feeling pleased or glad, including happy about and happy with.

**Original examples**

- I was happy to see our team reach the campsite before dark.
- The young scientist felt happy about her first successful experiment.
- Are you happy with the map you drew?

**Collocations**

- happy about the result
- happy with the plan
- happy to help
- make someone happy

**Word family and forms**

- happier → happiest (comparison; y changes to i)
- happiness (noun); happily (adverb)
- unhappy (adjective): not happy; un- changes the meaning

**Watch out:** Use happy to describe a person or feeling; use happily to describe an action: She is happy. She smiled happily. Say happy about the result or happy with the result, rather than happy of the result.

**Listening focus:** First-syllable stress, the /æ/ vowel, and a clear final /i/.

**Visual proposal:** Optional paired drawings of a satisfied explorer and a successful team experiment. Expressions alone are ambiguous, so show the reason for the feeling.

**Science extension proposal:** No science video is necessary. Ask how the same success might make different people feel; do not treat a facial expression as proof of someone's feelings.

## feather

countable noun · /ˈfɛðɚ/

**Definition:** One of the light, soft structures that cover a bird's body.

**Objective:** Identify one of the light structures covering a bird, and distinguish it from fur.

**Original examples**

- A gray feather floated onto the path.
- The eagle spread its long wing feathers before taking off.
- Soft feathers help the duck stay warm in cold water.

**Collocations**

- a bird's feather
- wing feathers
- soft feathers
- ruffle its feathers

**Word family and forms**

- feather → feathers (plural)
- feathered (adjective): covered with feathers
- feathery (adjective): soft or light like a feather

**Watch out:** Birds have feathers; most mammals have hair or fur. A feather is one individual structure, so say a feather or three feathers. Do not use a fur to mean one hair.

**Listening focus:** Voiced /ð/ in the middle, not /d/ or /θ/; American r-colored ending.

**Visual proposal:** An original labelled feather drawing showing the central shaft and branching parts, beside a small bird silhouette indicating wing and body feathers.

**Science extension proposal:** Compare a stiff wing feather with a soft down feather. Feathers can help with flight, warmth and display. Suggest a short licensed macro clip of feather branches; do not collect feathers from live birds.

## ribbon

countable noun · /ˈrɪbən/

**Definition:** A long, narrow strip of cloth used for tying or decorating things.

**Objective:** Understand ribbon as a narrow decorative strip of cloth and distinguish it from a bow.

**Original examples**

- She tied a blue ribbon around the gift box.
- A red ribbon marked the finish line of the race.
- We used a piece of ribbon to mark our place in the field guide.

**Collocations**

- a piece of ribbon
- tie a ribbon
- a satin ribbon
- a ribbon bow

**Word family and forms**

- ribbon → ribbons (plural)
- No useful beginner-level prefix or root is needed for this meaning. Learn the whole word rather than inventing parts.

**Watch out:** A ribbon is the strip of material; a bow is a shape made by tying it into loops. You can tie a ribbon into a bow. The word is spelled ribbon, with double b.

**Listening focus:** First-syllable stress, short /ɪ/, and the unstressed /ən/ ending.

**Visual proposal:** An original three-step diagram: a flat ribbon, the ribbon being tied, and the finished bow. Keep all three the same color so the material-shape relationship is clear.

**Science extension proposal:** Optional material comparison: a woven ribbon, paper strip and string. No science video is needed. Do not attach markers to trees or wildlife without permission.

## mineral

countable noun (geology sense) · /ˈmɪnɚəl/

**Definition:** A naturally occurring solid, such as quartz, with a particular chemical makeup and an ordered crystal structure.

**Objective:** Recognize a mineral by its natural origin, chemical makeup and crystal structure, and distinguish it from a rock.

**Original examples**

- Quartz is a common mineral found in many rocks.
- We examined the mineral sample with a hand lens.
- The geologist used several tests to identify the mineral.

**Collocations**

- a common mineral
- a mineral sample
- a mineral deposit
- identify a mineral

**Word family and forms**

- mineral → minerals (plural)
- mineral-rich (compound adjective): containing many minerals
- mineralogy (optional science noun): the study of minerals; do not split mineral into invented English roots

**Watch out:** A mineral is not the same as a rock: a rock may contain several minerals. Mineral in nutrition is a different sense; the calcium in food is not a tiny rock you should eat.

**Listening focus:** First-syllable stress; notice the unstressed middle and final syllables rather than stressing the end.

**Visual proposal:** An original comparison of one quartz mineral sample and a labelled granite diagram showing quartz, feldspar and mica. Avoid implying that every mineral looks clear or shiny.

**Science extension proposal:** Most minerals are inorganic. Some can form with help from living things, so where a sample was found is not enough to identify it. A real identification uses several properties, not color alone. Suggest a short adult-led hardness demonstration using safe samples; no acids or tasting.

## curious

adjective · /ˈkjʊriəs/

**Definition:** Wanting to learn or find out more about something.

**Objective:** Describe wanting to learn or find out something, using curious about and curious to know.

**Original examples**

- I was curious about the strange tracks beside the river.
- The curious students asked how the cave had formed.
- We were curious to know why the crystal had six sides.

**Collocations**

- curious about something
- curious to know
- a curious mind
- a curious child

**Word family and forms**

- curiously (adverb): in a way that shows interest
- curiosity (noun): a desire to know; learn the spelling change as a pair
- Do not teach curiosity as curious plus an unchanged suffix.

**Watch out:** Say curious about the tracks, rather than curious of the tracks. Curious can also mean unusual or strange, as in a curious noise; this lesson targets the wanting-to-learn meaning.

**Listening focus:** First-syllable stress, the /kj/ start, and a clear unstressed final /əs/.

**Visual proposal:** Optional scene of an explorer comparing tracks with a field guide and asking a question. The question and action communicate curiosity better than a face alone.

**Science extension proposal:** Turn a curious question into an observation: What do you notice? What could explain it? What safe evidence could you collect? No video is required.
